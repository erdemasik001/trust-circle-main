# Trust Circle — How the System Works

> A narrative walkthrough of the entire protocol, from a user's first touch to system-level defense mechanisms. Read this to understand not just *what* each piece does, but *why* it exists and how every part connects.

---

## The Core Idea in One Sentence

Trust Circle turns the social trust that already exists between people into programmable, enforceable credit — backed not by collateral, but by the reputation and capital of people who know you.

---

## Chapter 1: Identity — One Human, One Account

Everything begins with proof that you are a real, unique human being. Trust Circle uses **World ID 4.0** to verify this. When a user registers, they submit a zero-knowledge proof from the World ID protocol. The smart contract verifies this proof on-chain and records a unique nullifier hash. This nullifier ensures that no human can create a second account — ever.

Why does this matter? Because every economic attack on a social lending protocol starts with fake identities. If one person can create ten accounts, they can vouch for themselves, borrow against their own guarantees, default, and walk away with free money. World ID makes this impossible at the identity layer, before a single dollar moves.

Upon successful verification, the user receives an initial reputation score of **100 points**, placing them in the **Newcomer** tier. They also get an ENS L2 subname (e.g., `fatma.trustcircle.eth`) so that other users can find and vouch for them using a human-readable name instead of a hex address.

At this point, the user can borrow up to **$100** — but only if someone vouches for them first.

---

## Chapter 2: Vouching — Putting Money Where Your Mouth Is

Vouching is the beating heart of Trust Circle. When you vouch for someone, you are not just saying "I trust this person." You are **staking your own USDC** as a guarantee. If the borrower defaults, you lose real money.

### How a Vouch Works

1. **Voucher decides to back a borrower.** They call the vouch function, specifying the borrower's address and the USDC amount they are willing to stake. The minimum is **$10 USDC**.

2. **The vouch enters a 48-hour pending period.** It does not become active immediately. This delay exists to prevent flash-loan-style attacks where someone creates a vouch, borrows instantly, defaults, and disappears in a single transaction. The 48-hour window gives the system time to breathe and gives vouchers time to reconsider.

3. **After 48 hours, the vouch activates.** It now counts toward the borrower's available credit limit.

4. **The vouch amount is adjusted by a reputation multiplier.** Not all vouches are created equal. A voucher with high reputation (700+) has their stated amount multiplied by up to **1.5x** — the system trusts their judgment more. A voucher with low reputation (under 200) has their amount discounted to **0.5x**. This creates a natural hierarchy where experienced, reliable participants have more influence.

### Anti-Gaming Rules

- **No self-vouching.** You cannot vouch for yourself.
- **Maximum 5 active vouch positions per voucher.** This prevents a single wealthy actor from becoming a systemic risk point by backing too many borrowers.
- **40% concentration limit.** No single vouch can represent more than 40% of a borrower's total credit limit. This forces borrowers to build genuine social networks, not rely on a single whale.
- **Minimum voucher counts by tier.** Larger loans require more independent vouchers (3 for mid-tier, 5 for high-tier loans). This makes collusion between two people ineffective for large amounts.

### Why Vouching is Rational

A voucher earns **80% of the interest** generated by the loans they back. If a borrower at the Building tier borrows $2,000 at 8% interest for 30 days, the interest generated is approximately $13.15. The vouchers who backed that loan split 80% of it ($10.52) proportional to their stake. Over time, a selective voucher who backs trustworthy borrowers earns a consistent yield on their staked capital.

The math works out to positive expected value as long as the voucher is right about the borrower more than ~85% of the time. For people vouching for others they genuinely know and trust, this is a reasonable bar.

## Chapter 3: Borrowing — Tier-Gated, Risk-Priced Credit

Once a user has active vouches, they can borrow. But the protocol does not let them borrow arbitrary amounts at arbitrary rates. Everything is determined by their **reputation tier**.

### The Seven Tiers

| Tier | Rep Range | Max Borrow | Interest | Max Duration | Min Vouchers |
|------|-----------|------------|----------|--------------|--------------|
| Frozen | 0–99 | $0 | — | — | — |
| Newcomer | 100–199 | $100 | 15% | 14 days | 1 |
| Rising | 200–299 | $500 | 12% | 21 days | 3 |
| Building | 300–499 | $2,000 | 8% | 30 days | 3 |
| Trusted | 500–699 | $10,000 | 5% | 60 days | 3 |
| Established | 700–899 | $50,000 | 3% | 90 days | 5 |
| Leader | 900–1000 | $100,000 | 2% | 180 days | 5 |

A new user starts at Newcomer. They can only borrow $100 for 14 days at 15% interest. This is intentionally small. The first loan is a trust-building exercise, not a way to access serious capital. Each successful repayment adds **+10 reputation**, gradually unlocking higher tiers with better terms.

### What Happens When You Borrow

1. **Eligibility check.** The protocol verifies: the borrower's account is not frozen, they have no active loan, they have enough active vouches, the amount is within their tier limit, and the minimum voucher count is met.

2. **Circuit breaker check.** The protocol queries the CircuitBreaker contract. If the system is in RED health (too many recent defaults), new loans are blocked entirely. If YELLOW, the maximum borrow amount is cut by 50% and interest rates increase by 50%.

3. **Insurance contribution.** 1% of the loan principal is automatically routed to the InsurancePool. On a $1,000 loan, $10 goes to insurance. This funds the safety net that protects vouchers during defaults.

4. **Loan creation.** The remaining amount is transferred to the borrower. The loan records the principal, interest rate, due date, and which vouches back it.

5. **CircuitBreaker notification.** The system records that a new active loan exists, updating global health metrics.

The borrower now has USDC in their wallet and a clock ticking.

## Chapter 4: Repayment — The Happy Path

Most loans should end here. The borrower uses the funds, earns or receives money, and repays before the due date.

### Normal Repayment

The borrower calls the repay function with any amount of USDC. The protocol accepts partial repayments — the borrower can chip away at the debt over time. When the total repaid equals or exceeds the principal plus interest:

1. **Reputation reward.** The borrower gains **+10 reputation points**. Over many successful repayments, they climb tiers and unlock better terms.

2. **Yield distribution.** The interest portion is split: **80% to vouchers** (proportional to their stake) and **20% to the protocol treasury**.

3. **Vouch release.** The vouchers' staked USDC is unlocked and available for them to use or re-vouch.

4. **Overpayment refund.** If the borrower accidentally sends too much, the excess is automatically returned. This seems like a small detail, but it prevents a class of user-experience bugs that erode trust.

5. **CircuitBreaker notification.** The system records a successful repayment, which pushes global health metrics toward GREEN.

### Late Repayment (Grace Period)

If the due date passes and the loan is not fully repaid, the borrower enters a **7-day grace period**. During this window:

- A **2% late fee** is added to the outstanding amount.
- The borrower loses **10 reputation points** (but is not frozen).
- The borrower can still repay in full and avoid default.

The grace period exists because life is messy. Salaries arrive late. Banks have processing delays. Seven days is enough to handle genuine friction without letting bad actors stall indefinitely.

## Chapter 5: Default & Liquidation — The Unhappy Path

If the grace period expires and the loan remains unpaid, the loan enters **default status**. At this point, anyone — not just the borrower or vouchers — can trigger liquidation.

### What Happens During Liquidation

1. **Borrower punishment.**
   - **-50 reputation points.** For a Newcomer (starting at 100), this drops them to 50 — **Frozen**. They cannot borrow again until they rebuild.
   - **30-day cooldown.** Even after rebuilding reputation, the borrower must wait 30 days before receiving new vouches. This prevents rapid cycling.

2. **Voucher slashing.**
   - Vouchers lose up to **70% of their staked amount** proportional to the shortfall.
   - The InsurancePool covers up to **30% of each voucher's loss**. So if a voucher had $100 staked and the borrower defaulted fully, the voucher loses $70 and the insurance covers $30.

3. **Liquidation bounty.**
   - The person who triggers the liquidation receives a bounty paid from the InsurancePool. The bounty uses a **time-decay model**:
     - Days 1–3 after grace period: **1%** of outstanding debt
     - Days 4–7: **2%**
     - Days 8–14: **3%**
     - Days 15+: **5%**
   - This escalating bounty creates urgency. On day 1, the bounty might not be worth the gas cost. By day 15, it definitely is. This ensures that defaults are always settled eventually, without requiring a centralized liquidation bot.

4. **CircuitBreaker notification.** The system records the default. If too many defaults accumulate in a 7-day window, the system's health state may shift.

### Why Defaulting is Irrational

Consider a Newcomer who borrows $100. If they default:
- They lose all future access to credit (Frozen at rep 50).
- Their vouchers — real people who trusted them — lose money.
- They must spend 30 days in cooldown, then somehow convince new people to vouch for them with a visible default on their on-chain record.

Compare this to repaying: they pay back $100 + ~$1.15 interest, gain +10 reputation, and unlock access to $500 loans next time. The future value of continued access to credit vastly exceeds the cost of repayment at every tier. This is the Nash equilibrium: **Repay Always** dominates **Default** for any borrower who values future credit access.

## Chapter 6: Reputation — The Invisible Hand

Reputation is the mechanism that ties the entire system together. It is not a vanity metric or a gamification layer — it is the **price signal** that governs every interaction.

### Reputation Gains and Losses

| Event | Rep Change |
|-------|-----------|
| Successful repayment (on time) | +10 |
| Late repayment (within grace) | -10 |
| Default | -50 |
| Inactivity (after 90 days) | -5/month |

### Reputation Decay

If a user does nothing for 90 days, their reputation begins to decay at **5 points per month**. This prevents "reputation squatting" — someone who built a high score years ago should not have the same borrowing power as someone actively participating. The decay stops the moment the user makes any transaction (repayment, vouch, etc.).

### The Frozen State

If reputation drops below 100, the account is **Frozen**. A frozen user cannot borrow, cannot receive new vouches, and must actively participate (e.g., by vouching for others and earning reputation through the community) to climb back above 100.

This creates a recovery path that does not require admin intervention. A defaulter is not permanently banned — they can rebuild, but it takes genuine effort and time. The 30-day cooldown after default ensures they cannot immediately game their way back.

### Voucher Reputation Multiplier

The reputation of a voucher directly affects the credit they can extend:

| Voucher Rep | Multiplier | Effect |
|-------------|-----------|--------|
| 0–199 | 0.50x | $100 vouch counts as $50 |
| 200–499 | 0.75x | $100 vouch counts as $75 |
| 500–699 | 1.00x | $100 vouch counts as $100 |
| 700–899 | 1.25x | $100 vouch counts as $125 |
| 900–1000 | 1.50x | $100 vouch counts as $150 |

This means high-reputation vouchers are more "capital efficient." Their judgment is worth more because they have demonstrated it through consistent, reliable behavior. This naturally incentivizes vouchers to protect their own reputation — a voucher who backs a defaulter loses both money and reputation multiplier power.

## Chapter 7: The Insurance Pool — Collective Safety Net

The InsurancePool is funded automatically by a **1% contribution from every loan**. Nobody opts in or out; it is a protocol-level tax that builds a shared safety net.

### How It Works

- **Inflow:** 1% of every loan principal. A $10,000 loan contributes $100 to the pool.
- **Outflow (Coverage):** When a voucher is slashed during default, the pool covers up to **30% of their loss**. So voucher max loss = 70%, not 100%.
- **Outflow (Bounties):** Liquidation bounties are paid from the pool.

### Why It Matters

Without insurance, vouching is a high-risk activity with binary outcomes: you either earn yield or lose everything. With 30% insurance coverage, the worst case is losing 70% — still painful, but survivable. This makes the expected value of selective vouching clearly positive, which is critical for protocol adoption.

The pool is self-sustaining as long as the default rate stays below a mathematically determined threshold. The CircuitBreaker exists precisely to ensure this.

## Chapter 8: The Circuit Breaker — Systemic Defense

The CircuitBreaker monitors the protocol's health in real-time by tracking defaults within a **rolling 7-day window**. Based on the default rate (defaults / active loans), it assigns one of four health states:

### Health States

**GREEN** (default rate < 5%)
- Normal operation. All parameters at standard values.

**YELLOW** (default rate 10–20%)
- Maximum borrow amounts reduced by **50%** across all tiers.
- Interest rates increased by **50%** across all tiers.
- Rationale: reduce exposure and increase the cost of borrowing to slow down risky lending during stress.

**RED** (default rate >= 20%)
- **All new loans are blocked.** Nobody can borrow until the system recovers.
- Rationale: stop the bleeding. A 20% default rate is a death spiral if unchecked.

**RECOVERY** (returning from RED/YELLOW, default rate drops to <= 5%)
- Gradual normalization over **14 days**.
- System transitions back to GREEN after the recovery period elapses.
- Rationale: prevent whiplash. Immediately snapping back to full lending after a crisis invites the same crisis again.

### Why This Prevents Death Spirals

In traditional lending protocols, defaults cause fear, fear causes vouch withdrawals, withdrawals trigger more defaults, and the system collapses. The CircuitBreaker breaks this cycle by automatically tightening when stress appears. By the time a crisis could become self-reinforcing, the protocol has already slowed itself down.

## Chapter 9: Anti-Exploit Design — Closing Every Attack Vector

The 14-fix system is specifically designed to make every known attack vector unprofitable.

### Attack: Self-Vouching via Sybil Accounts
**Defense:** World ID ensures one human = one account. Creating a second account requires a second human.

### Attack: Flash Vouch-Borrow-Default
**Defense:** 48-hour vouch activation delay. You cannot create a vouch and use it in the same transaction or even the same day.

### Attack: Two-Person Collusion
**Defense:** Minimum voucher count (3–5 depending on loan size) and 40% concentration limit. Two people cannot generate enough credit for a meaningful loan. They would need 3–5 independent World ID-verified humans to collude, at which point the coordination cost likely exceeds the gain.

### Attack: Reputation Farming
**Defense:** Reputation gains are capped at +10 per repayment. Climbing from Newcomer (100) to Leader (900) requires 80 successful repayments. At 14 days per loan at Newcomer tier, reaching Leader takes over 3 years of continuous, successful borrowing. There is no shortcut.

### Attack: Grief (Vouching for Known Defaulters)
**Defense:** The voucher loses real money (70% of stake). Grief attacks cost the attacker more than the victim.

### Attack: Whale Domination
**Defense:** Max 5 vouch positions per voucher + 40% concentration limit. A whale's capital influence is structurally capped.

## Chapter 10: The Complete Flow — A Story

**Fatma** lives in Istanbul. She has a phone but no bank account, no credit score, no collateral.

1. **Day 0:** Fatma downloads the Trust Circle app and verifies her World ID. She is `fatma.trustcircle.eth` with 100 reputation (Newcomer).

2. **Day 1:** Her cousin **Mehmet** (reputation 300, Building tier) vouches for her with $50 USDC. The vouch enters the 48-hour pending state.

3. **Day 3:** The vouch activates. Mehmet's rep multiplier at 300 is 0.75x, so Fatma's effective credit limit is $37.50. She borrows $37 for 14 days at 15% interest. $0.37 goes to the InsurancePool. She receives $36.63.

4. **Day 15:** Fatma repays $37 + $0.43 interest = $37.43. She gains +10 reputation (now 110). Mehmet earns 80% of the interest ($0.34). His vouch is released.

5. **Day 16:** Fatma's neighbor **Ayse** (reputation 500, Trusted) sees Fatma's clean repayment history and vouches for her with $100 USDC. Ayse's multiplier is 1.0x. Combined with a renewed vouch from Mehmet ($50 at 0.75x = $37.50), Fatma's credit limit is now $137.50. She borrows $100.

6. **Months later:** After 10 successful repayments, Fatma has 200 reputation (Rising tier). She can now borrow up to $500, at 12% interest, for 21 days. Her borrowing power grows as her track record grows.

7. **One year later:** Fatma is at 400 reputation (Building tier). She can borrow $2,000 at 8% for 30 days. She uses this to buy inventory for her small business. She is now a participant in the formal credit economy — built entirely on the trust of people who know her.

## Chapter 11: System Architecture — The Contract Map

```
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │      TrustCircle (Main)       │
                    │                              │
                    │  • Registration (World ID)   │
                    │  • Vouching                  │
                    │  • Borrowing                 │
                    │  • Repayment                 │
                    │  • Liquidation               │
                    │  • User State Storage        │
                    └──────┬───┬───┬───┬───────────┘
                           │   │   │   │
              ┌────────────┘   │   │   └────────────┐
              ▼                ▼   ▼                ▼
     ┌────────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ ┌──────────────────┐
     │ Reputation     │ │  Insurance   │ │  Circuit         │
     │ Engine         │ │  Pool        │ │  Breaker         │
     │                │ │              │ │                  │
     │ • Tier lookup  │ │ • 1% inflow  │ │ • Health state   │
     │ • Rep decay    │ │ • 30% cover  │ │ • Loan tracking  │
     │ • Multiplier   │ │ • Bounties   │ │ • Rate adjust    │
     │ • Freeze check │ │              │ │ • Pause control  │
     └────────────────┘ └──────┬───────┘ └──────────────────┘
                               │
                          ┌────▼────┐
                          │  USDC   │
                          │ (ERC20) │
                          └─────────┘
```

The **TrustCircle** contract is the single entry point for all user interactions. It stores all user state (reputation scores, loan records, vouch positions) and delegates computation to three specialized contracts:

- **ReputationEngine** — Pure logic, no state. Given a reputation score, returns the tier, computes decay, and calculates voucher multipliers.
- **InsurancePool** — Holds USDC. Receives contributions, pays coverage and bounties. Only callable by the main TrustCircle contract.
- **CircuitBreaker** — Tracks loan and default counts. Determines system health. Adjusts protocol parameters during stress.

All money flows through **USDC** (6 decimals). There is no protocol token. This is intentional — a protocol token would add speculative dynamics that undermine the lending economics.

## Chapter 12: The Nash Equilibrium — Why It All Holds Together

Every design decision in Trust Circle serves one purpose: making **(Vouch Selectively, Repay Always)** the dominant strategy for every rational actor.

**For borrowers:** The future value of continued credit access always exceeds the cost of repaying the current loan. A $100 repayment unlocks $500 future access. A $500 repayment unlocks $2,000. Defaulting destroys this entire pipeline for a one-time gain that is always smaller than what is lost.

**For vouchers:** Selective vouching (backing people you genuinely know and trust) has positive expected value. The 80% yield share provides consistent returns. The 30% insurance coverage limits downside. The reputation multiplier rewards good judgment with more capital efficiency.

**For liquidators:** Triggering liquidation is always profitable once the bounty exceeds gas costs (which it does within a few days). No coordination needed — pure economic incentive ensures defaults are always resolved.

**For attackers:** Every attack vector costs more than it yields. Sybil attacks are blocked by World ID. Flash attacks are blocked by the 48-hour delay. Collusion is blocked by minimum voucher counts. Reputation farming is blocked by the years-long climb.

This is not hope. This is mechanism design. The protocol does not need users to be good people — it needs them to be rational. And rational actors, facing these incentives, will choose to cooperate. That is the Nash equilibrium.
